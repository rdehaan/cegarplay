Chapter 19 · Part II

More than two quantifier levels

Every loop so far had two levels: a candidate and a check. Problems with three alternating quantifiers, Σ3P and beyond, need a check that is itself a loop. This chapter shows two ways to build one: a chain of solvers, and a recursive procedure over a family of solvers.

A trilevel problem

Knapsack interdiction with fortification: a defender fortifies up to two items, an attacker removes up to two unfortified ones, and the defender packs what is left. A fortification is a solution when every attack leaves a packing that reaches the goal: ∃ fortification ∀ attack ∃ packing. Three solvers in a chain: A fortifies, B attacks, C packs.

Blocking at both levels

The middle level is a loop of its own: B proposes attacks for the current fortification, and C answers each with a packing or fails. The baseline blocks a refuted attack and a refuted fortification each on its own:

        loop:
            Y = B.solve(assume GF(M))
            if not Y: break
            K = C.solve(assume GA(Y))
            if K:
                B.add(GA.block(Y))
            else:
                reject M with GF.block(M)
                break

From the loop script of guide-19-fortify-block.

Clauses at both levels

Each level can learn as in Part I. A packing refutes an attack, and every attack that leaves the packed items alone is refuted by it too: "attack one of the packed items". An attack refutes a fortification, and every fortification that leaves the attacked items unprotected is refuted too: "fortify one of the attacked items". The library example knapsack-fortification uses these two clauses. It finds the 2 solutions after 7 fortifications and 21 inner calls, against 37 and 137 for blocking. Open knapsack-fortification Open the blocking loop

One subtlety: B's clauses must stay valid for later fortifications, because B keeps them. "Attack one of the packed items" does not mention the fortification, so it holds for every one. A clause of the middle level that named the outer candidate would have to name its atoms too, as the next section's loop does.

Cores along a chain

In QBF form (∃x ∀y ∃z), the innermost solver is a witness check, and a failure is explained by its core. The library example qbf-three-blocks searches B inside A's check and turns the x-part of C's core into a clause for A, CAQE-style: one solver per block, cores flowing outwards. It finds 4 solutions after 34 candidates. Open qbf-three-blocks

Any number of levels

For a formula with any number of blocks, write the check once as a procedure over a family of solvers S[1..depth] and links G. Level i answers a candidate of level i − 1 with a clause, or with none when it stands:

def refute(i, M):
    loop:
        Y = S[i].solve(assume G[i-1](M))
        if not Y: return none
        if i == S.size: return G[i-1].clause(Y)
        c = refute(i + 1, Y)
        if not c: return G[i-1].clause(Y) + S[i+1].core.on(G[i-1])
        S[i].add(c)

From the loop script of qbf-deep.

This is RAReQS's recursion with clauses in place of expansion. With three blocks the example's formula is false: the loop proves it after 4 candidates; with four blocks (depth = 4) after 4 candidates and 36 inner calls. Open qbf-deep

Exercise 1

In the fortification loop, B's clause "attack one of the packed items" is learnt inside the check of one fortification and kept for all later ones. Why is that sound, and which clause would not be?

Solution

A packing that avoids the attacked items exists whatever is fortified, so every attack that leaves those items alone is answered by it, for every fortification: the clause removes no attack that could still win. A clause that used the current fortification (for example "attack an item that is not fortified now") would be valid only for this candidate; B would have to forget it, or name the fortification's atoms in it, before the next candidate. Recap

  • With three levels the check is a loop: a chain of solvers, each refining the one before.
  • A clause learnt by an inner level must stay valid for every outer candidate, or name the outer atoms it depends on.
  • For any number of levels, a recursive procedure over a solver family writes the loop once.
